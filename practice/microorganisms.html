Section one

Four words worth knowing

Science has a habit of using a long word for a simple thing. These four come up constantly, so it is worth getting them straight once rather than guessing every time.

A. A tiny living thing you can only see with a microscope.

B. A germ that can make people ill.

C. A microbe that rots dead leaves and plants.

D. A medicine that kills bad bacteria.

Worth noticing: only one of these four words means something harmful. People use "germ" for all of them, which is a bit like calling every animal a wasp.

Section two

The three main groups

Nearly everything microscopic falls into one of three groups: bacteria, fungi and viruses. They are not variations on the same thing. They are about as similar to each other as a dog, a mushroom and a set of instructions.

Bacteria are single living cells. They eat, they grow, they split in two to make more. Most of them are getting on with their lives and have no opinion about you. A few thousand of your species-mates got ill from a handful of the nasty ones, and now the whole group has a reputation.

Fungi includes yeast and mould, and also mushrooms, which are just the visible bit of something much larger underground. Yeast has been employed by humans for thousands of years and has never once been paid.

Viruses are the strange ones. A virus is barely alive. It is instructions in a shell, and it can do precisely nothing until it gets inside a living cell and makes that cell build copies of it. Which is, when you think about it, quite rude.

Section three

The useful ones

Microbes turn up in a surprising amount of what you eat. Bread, yoghurt, cheese, chocolate, soy sauce, anything pickled, and every fizzy drink that was ever brewed. If you removed microorganisms from the food supply, the shops would be extremely dull.

Here is what is actually happening in that bowl. Yeast is alive. Put it somewhere warm with flour and water and it starts eating the sugars in the dough. What it breathes out is carbon dioxide, which is a gas, and the gas gets trapped in the stretchy dough and pushes it up from the inside. Every hole in a slice of bread is a bubble a fungus breathed out. You have been eating that your entire life.

Penicillin, the first proper antibiotic, was found because Alexander Fleming left a dish out, went on holiday, came back to mould growing on it, and noticed the bacteria had died in a ring around the mould. The single most useful accident in medicine happened because somebody did not do the washing up.

Section four

The clean-up crew

Everything that has ever died is still here, in some form. The only reason you are not standing on several thousand years of unrotted leaves, dead trees and deceased pigeons is that bacteria and fungi in the soil eat all of it.

They are called decomposers. They break dead matter down into nutrients, the nutrients go back into the soil, plants take them up and grow, and something eats the plant. Nothing is thrown away because there is nowhere to throw it. It just goes round.

Section five

Germs, and the twenty seconds nobody does

Handwashing is the least impressive piece of medical science ever discovered and one of the most effective. When it was first suggested that doctors should wash their hands between patients, the doctors were furious about the insult. It took decades to catch on, and an enormous number of people died in the meantime because of professional pride.

Soap works in a genuinely interesting way. It is not poison. Soap molecules prise apart the greasy outer layer that a lot of germs are wrapped in, and the water carries the wreckage down the drain. That is why it takes twenty seconds: the prising takes a moment.

It sounds reasonable and it is completely wrong, and being able to say why, calmly, with two examples, is most of what scientific thinking actually is.
