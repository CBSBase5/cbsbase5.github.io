BOUNDARIES AND SIGNALS

PHSE

How to tell whether somebody is interested, what to do when they are not, and how to turn somebody down without being cruel.

Nobody teaches this properly. People are expected to work it out from television, which is written by adults who are guessing. So here it is written down.

Six sections, tap rather than write, and it tells you as you go. Start anywhere, stop anywhere, and it saves itself. Nothing reaches Jay unless you press the button at the end.

Fair warning that this one is about liking people and being turned down, which for most people is not a neutral subject. Skipping a bit you do not fancy today is allowed and always was.

Section C

Just asking

Alex notices that Jordan smiles every time they pass in the corridor. Alex is now conducting a full investigation into the meaning of this smile, which is what everybody does, and it never works.

There is no way to decode a smile. You could watch somebody for a year and still not know. The only method that actually works is embarrassingly simple: ask, plainly, once, in a way that gives them an easy way out.

Question 5c

Which is the best question for Alex to ask?

Notice what the good version does. It says what Alex noticed, it offers two answers, and one of those answers is just being friendly. That last bit is the whole trick: you are handing them a way to say no that costs them nothing. People answer honestly far more often when the honest answer is not going to cause a scene.

Section D

Saying no, and being told it

You never have to say yes to anybody, for any reason, including no reason. Not to be nice, not because they were brave to ask, not because everyone thinks you should. That is not a rule with exceptions in it.

The kind version is short, clear and not vague. Vague feels gentler and is actually crueller, because it leaves the other person to keep guessing.

Now the other side of it. Being turned down stings, and anybody who says it does not is lying. What it is not is a verdict on you as a person. It is one person's answer about one thing on one day.

What people remember is not that you asked. It is what you did in the ten seconds after the answer.

Way one: accept their choice calmly and do not with them.
Way two: give them and treat them normally at school.

Section E

Leo and Mia

Leo and Mia are in the same tutor group. Leo likes Mia and wants to be more than friends.

Monday

Leo sits next to Mia at lunch. Mia smiles politely, then turns round to talk to somebody else.

Tuesday

Leo sends three messages asking Mia to the cinema. Two hours later Mia replies: "Can't, got homework."

Wednesday

Leo waits by Mia's locker and asks again. Mia looks down, steps back, and says she has to go.

Leo thinks: "Mia has not said no, so I will keep asking until she says yes."

Leo is not a villain. He is doing the thing films taught him to do, where persistence is romantic and eventually wins. In real life that is the point where an awkward situation turns into one that frightens somebody.

The line is not about how much you like somebody, it is about what you do after the signals arrive. Asking once is fine. Asking again after a no, turning up where they will be, getting other people involved: that is the point where it stops being about your feelings and starts being about theirs. And if somebody is doing this to you, that is worth telling Jay or another adult about, not because you cannot handle it but because you should not have to.
